Cycling Power Zones Explained (All 7 Zones)

What each zone actually trains, how it feels, and how to use them — without the sports-science headache.

Power zones are just ranges of effort, each defined as a percentage of your FTP (the power you can hold for about an hour). They exist so that "ride easy" and "ride hard" become specific, repeatable targets instead of guesses. The most common system, from Dr. Andrew Coggan, has seven zones. Here's each one in plain English.

491–105%ThresholdRight at your limit; no chatting

What each zone does

Zone 1 — Active recovery

Easy spinning that promotes blood flow without adding fatigue. Used on recovery days and between hard intervals. If it feels like exercise, you're going too hard.

Zone 2 — Endurance

The foundation of everything. Builds your aerobic engine, teaches your body to burn fat, and lets you ride longer and recover faster. Should be the majority of your weekly time. More on this in Zone 2 Training.

Zone 3 — Tempo

"Comfortably hard." Useful for building endurance strength and simulating steady climbing or group-ride pace. Handy, but easy to overuse — too much Zone 3 is the classic "grey zone" trap that leaves you tired without being fast.

Zone 4 — Threshold

Riding at your ceiling. This is where you directly push your FTP up. Demanding, so one or two focused sessions a week is plenty. See Sweet Spot vs. Threshold.

Zone 5 — VO2 max

Short, hard 3–8 minute efforts that raise your aerobic ceiling — which pulls your FTP up from the top. They hurt and they're potent; best used in focused blocks.

Zones 6 & 7 — Anaerobic & sprint

Very short, very high power. Trains your ability to attack, bridge, and sprint. Important for racing and punchy rides; less critical if your goal is a steady gran fondo or century.

Zones are the "what." A plan is the "when."

How to actually use them

You don't train every zone every week. The proven approach for most riders is polarized-ish: spend the large majority of time in Zone 2, add a focused dose of Zone 4–5 work, and keep the middle (Zone 3) as the exception rather than the default. Roughly 80% easy, 20% hard. That's what turns zones from trivia into fitness.

Do I need a power meter?

Power zones are most precise with a power meter, but you can approximate them with heart rate (it lags and drifts, but works) or even perceived effort using the "feels like" column above. Start with what you have; the discipline of riding to a target matters more than the precision of the target.
