Getting Started

The Beginner's Guide to Structured Cycling Training

You've been riding for a while. Here's how to stop just riding — and start actually getting faster.

Most cyclists improve quickly at first, then plateau. They ride the same routes at the same medium effort and wonder why they're not getting faster. The answer is structure: instead of riding however you feel, you ride with a purpose each day. It sounds intimidating and expensive, but the core ideas are simple. Here's everything a beginner needs to start.

What "structured training" actually means

It just means each ride has a specific job. Some days you go genuinely easy to build endurance; a couple of days you do focused hard efforts (intervals) to raise your ceiling; and you arrange them so you recover between the hard ones. That's it. The structure is what turns random miles into steady, measurable progress.

Step 1: Find your baseline

Almost everything in structured training is anchored to your FTP — the power you can hold for about an hour. You don't need a lab; a simple 20-minute test (or an estimate from a recent hard effort) gets you a starting number. Use our free FTP calculator to get yours in seconds.

Step 2: Learn the two ingredients

  • Easy rides (Zone 2). Conversational pace. Build your aerobic base. This should be most of your riding. See Why slow rides make you fast.
  • Hard efforts (intervals). Structured blocks like sweet spot or threshold that push your fitness up. One or two per week.

The proven balance is roughly 80% easy, 20% hard. The most common beginner mistake is doing everything at "medium" — too hard to build a base, too easy to improve.

Step 3: A simple first week (5–6 hours)

DayRide
MonRest
TueIntervals — 3 × 10 min at sweet spot (~90% FTP)
WedEasy 45–60 min (Zone 2) or rest
ThuIntervals — 4 × 5 min at threshold (~100% FTP)
FriRest
SatLong easy ride (your longest of the week)
SunEasy 45 min or off

Repeat and progress it for 3 weeks — add a little each week — then take an easier recovery week before building again.

Step 4: Recover on purpose

You don't get faster during workouts — you get faster while recovering from them. That means easy days need to be easy, sleep matters, and you should eat enough to fuel your training. Skipping recovery is how beginners end up tired all the time and stuck.

Common beginner questions

  • Do I need a power meter? It's ideal, but heart rate or even perceived effort works to start. Don't let gear stop you.
  • How many hours a week? Even 4–6 focused hours produces real gains. Consistency beats volume.
  • How fast will I improve? Newer riders often see meaningful gains in 6–8 weeks. Enjoy it — the early progress is the fastest you'll ever get.

The takeaway

Structured training isn't complicated or only for racers. Test your FTP, ride mostly easy with a couple of hard sessions, progress gradually, and recover well. Do that consistently and you'll break through the plateau that keeps most riders stuck for years.
