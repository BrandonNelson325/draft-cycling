How to Train for a Century Ride (100 Miles)

Whether it's your first hundred or your fastest, here's how to arrive ready instead of hoping to survive.

A century — 100 miles (or 100 km for a metric century) — is one of cycling's great goals: far enough to demand real preparation, achievable for almost anyone who trains for it. The riders who finish strong aren't necessarily the fittest; they're the ones who built endurance progressively and dialed in their fueling. Here's how to do both.

How long do you need?

  • Already riding regularly (2–3× a week): 8–12 weeks of focused build.
  • Newer / returning: 12–16 weeks, starting from shorter rides.
  • The key requirement: you should comfortably ride 3–4 hours before event day. You don't need to ride the full 100 in training.

The weekly structure

Three rides a week gets most people there; four is better if you have time:

RidePurpose
1 long ride (the priority)Build endurance — extend it gradually week to week
1 intensity rideSweet spot or tempo, so climbs and headwinds don't crush you
1–2 easy ridesZone 2 to add volume and aid recovery

The long ride is everything

Extend your longest ride by roughly 10–15% each week, then pull it back every 3rd or 4th week to recover. Build toward a longest ride of about 65–80 miles (or 3.5–5 hours). That's enough to prove your endurance and dial in fueling without needing to ride the whole distance and dig a fatigue hole you can't climb out of before event day.

Train for the actual route

A flat century and a climbing century are completely different events. If your ride has 8,000 ft of climbing, your long rides need hills; if it's pancake-flat, practice holding steady power for hours. This is exactly why route-specific training matters — the demands of your event should shape the plan.

Fueling: where most centuries are won or lost

  • Aim for 60–90g of carbs per hour once you're riding longer than ~90 minutes. That's roughly 1–2 gels or a bottle of mix plus a bar each hour.
  • Eat and drink before you feel like you need to. Once you're hungry or thirsty, you're already behind.
  • Practice your exact fueling on long training rides. Never try something new on event day.

The final two weeks: taper

Resist the urge to cram. In the last 1–2 weeks, cut your volume by 30–50% while keeping a little intensity to stay sharp. You'll feel almost lazy — that's the point. You're shedding fatigue so your months of fitness can finally show up. (See Fitness, Fatigue & Form.)

Event-day checklist

  • Start slower than feels right — the first hour should feel too easy.
  • Eat on a schedule, every 30–45 minutes.
  • Sit in with other riders when you can; drafting saves enormous energy.
  • Nothing new: same bike setup, same food, same kit you trained on.
