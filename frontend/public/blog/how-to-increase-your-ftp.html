How to Increase Your FTP: A Practical Guide

The training that actually raises your threshold power — what to do, how often, and the mistakes that keep most riders stuck.

Your FTP — Functional Threshold Power, the highest power you can hold for about an hour — is the number your whole training revolves around. Raise it and every zone moves up with it: your endurance pace gets faster, your climbs get easier, and you hold the wheel in the group longer. The good news is that FTP is very trainable. The catch is that most riders raise it slowly (or not at all) because they train hard in the wrong way.

Here's what actually works, without the jargon.

First, know your starting number

You can't tell if your FTP is going up if you never measured it. Do a simple field test — a 20-minute all-out effort works well — and take 95% of your average power. If you'd rather not suffer through a full test, you can estimate it from a shorter effort.

The workouts that actually raise FTP

Threshold power responds best to a mix of work slightly below, at, and above your current FTP — plus a big base of easy riding underneath it. In rough order of how much bang-for-buck they give a time-crunched rider:

1. Sweet spot (88–94% of FTP)

The efficiency champion. Sweet spot sits just below threshold — hard enough to drive real adaptation, easy enough that you can accumulate a lot of it without wrecking yourself. Sessions like 3 × 12 minutes or 2 × 20 minutes are the backbone of most FTP-building blocks. If you only have a few hours a week, this is where most of your intensity should live. (More on this in Sweet Spot vs. Threshold.)

2. Threshold intervals (95–105% of FTP)

Riding right at your ceiling teaches your body to clear lactate at higher and higher power. Think 3 × 10 minutes or 2 × 15 minutes at or just above FTP. These are demanding — one, maybe two per week is plenty.

3. VO2 max intervals (106–120% of FTP)

Short, hard efforts — 5 × 3 minutes or 6 × 4 minutes — lift the ceiling above your threshold, which pulls FTP up from the top. They hurt, and they're most useful in focused blocks rather than year-round.

4. Zone 2 endurance (56–75% of FTP)

The quiet hero. Easy, conversational rides build the aerobic engine that lets you recover between hard efforts and hold power longer. This should be the majority of your weekly time. Skip it and your hard days have no foundation to stand on.

The most common reason riders plateau: every ride is medium-hard. Too hard to build a base, too easy to drive adaptation. Make your easy days truly easy so your hard days can be truly hard.

How to put it together (the 80/20 rule)

Decades of research and coaching converge on a simple ratio: roughly 80% of your training time easy, 20% hard. For a rider with 6 hours a week that might look like:

DaySession
TueSweet spot — 3 × 12 min @ 90%
ThuThreshold or VO2 — 3 × 10 min @ 100% / 5 × 3 min @ 115%
SatLong endurance ride — Zone 2
SunEasy endurance or recovery spin
Mon / Wed / FriRest or very easy

Two quality sessions and one long easy ride is enough to drive steady gains for most amateurs. More intensity is not better — recovery is when you actually get stronger.

Progressive overload: the part everyone skips

Your body adapts to what you repeatedly ask of it, so the ask has to keep growing. Add a little each week — an extra interval, a few more minutes at intensity, or a few more watts — for about three weeks, then take an easier recovery week before building again. That build-build-build-recover rhythm is what separates riders who improve for years from those who spin their wheels.

How long until my FTP goes up?

If you're newer to structured training, you can see meaningful gains in 6–8 weeks — sometimes a jump of 10–20+ watts in a first block. The more trained you already are, the slower and smaller the gains, and the more they depend on consistency and smart recovery. Retest every 4–6 weeks; testing more often just measures your fatigue.

Common mistakes that keep your FTP flat

  • Riding everything at "medium." The grey-zone trap. Polarize your intensity instead.
  • Never doing easy rides easy. No recovery, no adaptation.
  • Skipping recovery weeks. Fitness is built during rest, not during the intervals.
  • Chasing a new workout every week. Repeating and progressing the same sessions is what drives adaptation.
  • Under-fueling. You can't adapt to training you didn't recover from, and you can't recover without eating.
  • Testing too often. Let the training accumulate before you measure again.

The bottom line

Raising your FTP isn't complicated: build a big easy base, add a focused dose of sweet spot and threshold work, progress it gradually, recover hard, and repeat. The hardest part is doing it consistently and knowing how to adjust when a week goes sideways — which is exactly the part a good coach (or a good coaching app) handles for you.
